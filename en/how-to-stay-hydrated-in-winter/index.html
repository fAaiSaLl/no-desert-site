Do You Need Less Water in Winter? How to Stay Hydrated When It's Cold

Last reviewed: 2026-11-25

Short answer: not really. Your body still needs a steady supply of fluid when it's cold, but you tend to feel less thirsty, so it's easier to fall behind without noticing. The fix is simple: drink on a routine instead of waiting for thirst, and count warm drinks and soups too.

Why you feel less thirsty in winter

In summer, heat and sweat make thirst hard to ignore. In winter the signal gets quieter. Researchers who study thirst have found that cold exposure can lower how thirsty people feel even when their body is a little short on water, and that older adults tend to feel less thirst in general. In other words, the feeling of thirst is a weaker guide when it's cold outside.

At the same time, you keep losing water in ways that are easy to miss:

  • Dry indoor heat. Heated air is dry, and you lose water through your skin and breath all day.
  • Breathing cold air. Your body warms and moistens every breath, and some of that moisture leaves with each exhale (the "smoke" you see on a cold day).
  • Sweat under layers. Shoveling snow or a brisk walk in a heavy coat still makes you sweat, even if you don't feel it.
  • More trips to the bathroom. Cold makes the body pull blood toward its core, and many people notice they pee more when they're chilled.

How much fluid do you need?

There's no single number for everyone. As a general guide, Mayo Clinic cites a daily total fluid intake of about 15.5 cups (3.7 liters) for men and 11.5 cups (2.7 liters) for women. That total includes all drinks and food, and Mayo notes that food usually provides about 20% of daily water. Your own needs depend on your size, activity, health, medications and whether you're pregnant or breastfeeding.

For a rough personal estimate, see how much water to drink a day by weight. More is not always better: drinking far more than you need can lower blood sodium (hyponatremia), so aim for steady, not extreme.

Signs you're running low

A quick check that works in any season is your urine. Mayo Clinic says that if you rarely feel thirsty and your urine is colorless or light yellow, your intake is probably adequate. Other common signs of mild dehydration include:

  • Dark yellow urine or peeing less often than usual
  • Dry mouth, dry lips or headache
  • Feeling tired, dizzy or lightheaded

Severe dizziness, confusion, a racing heart or not peeing at all need prompt medical care.

7 practical ways to drink enough when it's cold

  1. Drink on a schedule, not on thirst. A glass when you wake up, one with each meal and one mid-afternoon covers a lot without thinking about it.
  2. Go warm. Warm water with lemon, herbal tea, tea or coffee all count as fluid. At normal amounts tea and coffee don't dehydrate you (more in does tea count as water intake?).
  3. Eat your water. Soups, stews, broth, oranges and clementines all add fluid.
  4. Keep a bottle where you sit. At your desk, by the couch, in the car. If it's in sight, you'll sip.
  5. Drink around winter workouts. Have some water before and after a run, ski day or snow shoveling, even if you don't feel sweaty.
  6. Pace drinks at holiday parties. Alternate alcohol with water. CDC/NIOSH cold-stress guidance also advises avoiding alcohol when you're out in the cold and choosing warm, sweet drinks instead.
  7. Watch older family members. Older adults feel thirst less. Offering a warm drink at set times can help.

FAQ

Is it possible to get dehydrated in winter?

Yes. You still lose water through breathing, dry indoor air, sweat and urine, and because you feel less thirsty you may not replace it.

Does cold water or warm water hydrate better?

Both hydrate you. Pick the temperature that makes you drink more; in winter that's often warm.

Should I drink the same amount as in summer?

Probably a bit less if you sweat less, but not dramatically less. Let urine color guide you, and drink more on active days.

When should I talk to a doctor?

If you have heart, kidney or liver disease, take diuretics, or were told to limit fluids, ask your doctor how much you should drink. The general numbers above may not apply to you.
